## How to Read This Document

Before the story begins, five tools will make every page that follows understandable. None of them requires legal training, and each is used throughout.

**1. The tier system.** Every factual claim in this document carries a pedigree tag, because not all evidence is equal, and a reader deserves to know exactly what kind of thing they are looking at. *S-tier* means a primary document — a court filing, a transcript, a bank record, a signed affidavit, a recorded vote, a statute's text. *A-tier* means reporting by a major news organization that quotes or links those documents directly. *B-tier* means credible reporting that paraphrases without full sourcing. *C-tier* means a single-source or contested claim, always explicitly flagged. Where those categories are not fine enough, three labels are used inside the text: *T1* for a documented fact, *T2* for an attributed claim or a clearly-labeled analysis, and *T3* for speculation or debunked material, which is retained anyway — because even a false claim is evidence of something: of the pressure, the incentives, or the fog that produced it.

**2. The vocabulary.** An **FD-302** is the FBI's standard form for memorializing an interview — an agent's written summary of what a witness said, drafted after the conversation. A 302 records an allegation; it does not verify one, and the distinction runs through everything in this document. A **SAR** is a Suspicious Activity Report — the filing banks are legally required to make to the Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network when they detect transactions that might signal crime, expected promptly. As you will see, the question of *when* these banks filed is one of the central scandals in the record. An **NPA** is a non-prosecution agreement — the 2008 deal that closed the first federal case against Jeffrey Epstein and, in a provision drafted by his defense lawyers, immunized unnamed "potential co-conspirators" in perpetuity. **EFTA** is the Epstein Files Transparency Act, Public Law 119-38, signed November 19, 2025, which ordered the Justice Department to release essentially everything it held, subject to five narrow exceptions, on a thirty-day clock. A **Bates number** — the "EFTA" prefixes you will see throughout — is a unique tracking stamp on every page the government releases; the gaps between them are themselves evidence. A **proffer** is a conditional interview in which a witness's statements cannot be used directly against her. A **discharge petition** is the House device that forces a floor vote on a bill the leadership refuses to bring up, provided a majority of members signs it. **OCDETF** is the Organized Crime Drug Enforcement Task Forces program, the Justice Department's multi-agency money-trafficking umbrella under which one of the forgotten investigations in this record ran for nine years. **A voluntary undertaking** is the legal theory at the heart of the Florida mass-tort suits: once the FBI accepted Maria Farmer's 1996 report and told her to tell no one else, the plaintiffs argue, it assumed a duty by its actions and then failed to perform it. **The zone-of-risk doctrine** is its companion: those who create a danger can owe a duty to those they leave exposed. **The discretionary-function exception** is the government's shield against such suits — agencies cannot be sued over policy judgments, only over failures to follow their own mandatory rules, which is why the survivors' discovery demands focus on whether any mandatory rules existed. **A GRAT** — grantor-retained annuity trust — is an estate-tax device that pays its creator an annuity for a set term and passes the remainder to heirs; the $141 million "overpayment" in the Black file is money that mechanism moved. **Structuring**, in banking law, is breaking a large cash need into transactions just below the $10,000 reporting threshold — a federal crime in itself, and the precise pattern the regulator found in ninety-seven withdrawals of exactly $7,500. **Correspondent banking** is the plumbing that lets a New York bank move money to Moscow through a local partner; it is how the Russian wires ran without either bank holding the other's accounts. **An order to show cause** is a court's demand that a party justify itself or face sanctions — the "OSC" that punctuated the Phang summer. **A nolle prosequi** is the formal abandonment of a criminal prosecution — the Latin tag for the August 29, 2019 paper that closed the Epstein indictment without a verdict. **A donor-advised fund** is a charity-within-a-charity that lets a donor take the tax deduction now and dribble grants out later — the vessel Project Molecule proposed to build at $100 billion scale. **Layering** is money-laundering's middle step: moving value through shells and conversions until its origin dissolves.

**3. The cast, briefly.** Jeffrey Epstein: the financier, convicted in 2008 of soliciting prostitution from a minor, indicted again in July 2019 on federal sex-trafficking charges, dead in his Manhattan jail cell on August 10, 2019. Ghislaine Maxwell: his closest associate, convicted in December 2021 of sex trafficking, sentenced to twenty years, and as of this writing the holder of a clemency request the Senate unanimously voted to oppose. The *victims*: more than a thousand women and girls by the government's own estimates, over a hundred of whom have received settlements from his estate. The *institutions*: banks, universities, two presidential administrations, Congress, the courts, a royal family, and the Federal Bureau of Prisons. And the *audience*: you.

**4. Why this document exists.** On October 18, 2026, a Spanish-language post on X shared a short video of Congressman Dan Goldman speaking on the House floor. The post claimed, in blunt terms, that Goldman had FBI receipts proving Donald Trump assaulted a thirteen-year-old girl; that the girl bit him in self-defense; that the FBI interviewed her four times while burying her case; and that the Attorney General of the United States lied to Congress about all of it. Tens of millions of people have now seen fragments of this claim in one form or another. Almost none of them have seen the underlying documents, and almost everything viral about the story — in both directions — misdescribes what those documents actually say and what has happened around them in the nineteen months since Jeffrey Epstein's files became a political earthquake. This document is the underlying record, assembled in full: the accusers and their statements, the investigators and their depositions, the money and its every documented wire, the releases and their every documented failure, the courtroom fight now pending, and the governments abroad now prosecuting their own. The exculpatory material sits beside the accusatory, the counter-evidence beside the evidence, and the author's theories nowhere at all.

**5. One warning about human nature.** The Epstein story has been compared to a Rorschach test — people look at it and see confirmation of whatever they already believed about power, about the other political party, about the media, about intelligence agencies. The method of this document is the opposite. It follows a rule drawn from investigative tradecraft: *collect clues, don't adopt theories.* When a source lies, the lie is documented as a lie and the source's true statements are kept. When a document is authentic but its contents unverified, both facts are stated. When the government and a bank tell two irreconcilable stories, both stories are printed and the conflict is flagged rather than resolved by assertion. The truth about this case is not a team sport, and this document plays no position.

## Part I — The Spark

### Chapter 1: A Ninety-Second Video

On the evening of Sunday, October 18, 2026, an account on X calling itself *liderfiscal* — "Valy," with a hat-and-masks emoji pair in its display name — posted a video just over a minute long. The post's text, in Spanish, made five claims in rapid succession, capped by a hashtag. First, that Congressman Dan Goldman of New York was standing on the floor of the House of Representatives holding "FBI Form 302 receipts." Second, that the documents described Donald Trump sexually assaulting a girl of thirteen. Third, that the girl "bit him in self defense." Fourth, that the FBI interviewed her four times "while the case was being buried," and that the Attorney General, Pam Bondi, knew and lied to Congress — "no hay evidencia," the post quoted her — which the post labeled, in capital letters, perjury and a cover-up. Fifth, beneath all of it: #TrumpIsAPedophile.

The date and hour of the post are knowable to the minute, and the method is worth explaining because it is the same method used throughout this document. Social media platforms assign every post a unique numeric identifier, and those identifiers are not random: on X, they are generated by a system that embeds the exact time of creation inside the number, the way tree rings encode a tree's age. Decoding the identifier on this post yields 8:37 PM Eastern Time on October 18, 2026 — roughly twenty-three hours before this sentence was written [S-tier: snowflake-ID decode, the same method researchers use to date any X post].

The video inside the post was not new, and that fact matters more than the post's virality suggests. It showed Goldman speaking in the House of Representatives on March 18, 2026 — a five-minute special-order speech of the kind members deliver to an almost-empty chamber after the day's business has ended, in the full knowledge that the real audience is the clipping, sharing, archiving internet. When the video recirculated in mid-October 2026 with an #URGENTE framing and the hashtags of the moment, it was seven months old. Everything that had happened in those seven months — the releases, the rulings, the firings, the depositions, the contempt votes, the foreign prosecutions, and the slow public digestion of more than three million pages — is what turned a floor speech into a global event. That is the rest of this document.

To understand what the post claimed, and what it got right and wrong, it is necessary first to reconstruct exactly what happened on the floor that day, word by recorded word.

### Chapter 2: March 18, 2026 — The Speech, In Full

The House chamber on Wednesday, March 18, 2026, was largely empty, as it usually is for five-minute speeches. The Congressional Record for that day — Volume 172, Number 49 — carries the speech under the heading "EPSTEIN FILES," and records the format precisely: "Mr. GOLDMAN of New York was recognized to address the House for 5 minutes" [S-tier: Congressional Record, H2557].

Goldman, a former federal prosecutor from New York — the son, grandson, and great-grandson of famously litigious New Yorkers, himself the prosecutor who had won the first conviction in the Theranos case before running for Congress — rose and began:

> "Mr. Speaker, I rise today to discuss this administration's massive coverup of the Epstein files in order to conceal President Trump's involvement. This coverup reached its apex last month when Attorney General Pam Bondi falsely testified under oath before the House Committee on the Judiciary. The Attorney General testified: 'There is no evidence that Donald Trump has committed a crime. Everyone knows that.' This is a lie."

The Record shows what came next, and it is worth setting out at length because nearly everything the viral post claimed traces to this passage. Goldman told the chamber that in the roughly three million documents the Justice Department had disclosed to Congress under the Act, "there is evidence that Donald Trump committed a crime" — and that this figure excluded "the nearly 3 million pages that the DOJ is refusing to turn over." He said Trump was "mentioned thousands of times in the Epstein files," that "there are multiple allegations that he sexually assaulted young girls," and that he wanted to focus on one victim's account — "one victim's allegations that the FBI unquestionably found to be credible."

He then described the interview record. The FBI, he said, had "tried to hide reports of three of the four interviews that this victim gave to the FBI" until Democrats forced their disclosure. The first full interview in which she described Donald Trump occurred on August 7, 2019, and Goldman read the agent's summary aloud, in the Record's sanitized rendering:

> The victim "explained that she was between 13 and 15 when she was left alone with Donald Trump, who 'mentioned something to the effect of, Let me teach you how little girls are supposed to be. Trump unzipped his pants and put [her] head down, down to his penis. [The victim] bit the [s—] out of it. Trump struck [the victim] and said words to the effect of: Get this little [b—] the hell out of here. [The victim] advised she bit Trump's penis because he disgusted her."

A third interview, about two weeks later, added "additional details." A fourth, a month after that, ended when the woman asked, in Goldman's rendering, "what the point would be of providing the information at this point in her life when there was a strong possibility nothing could be done about it."

Goldman then read the supporting record into the Record. The FBI, he said, "created a PowerPoint presentation for prosecutors summarizing these allegations" — the 21-page presentation that had become, by then, one of the most fought-over documents in Washington. In addition to this account, he noted, "another victim alleged that Epstein introduced her to Donald Trump when she was 14 years old. He said, 'This is a good one, right?' Both of them chuckled. This victim said that Trump 'got the best of me that day.'"

Then the exculpatory record, read aloud so it could not be said to be hidden: "Now it is true that Trump denied these allegations." And then the reasons Goldman gave for finding the denials not credible, item by item, each one documented elsewhere in this record and each cross-referenced in its proper place below:

The 2002 New York Magazine quotation, read verbatim: "I've known Jeff for 15 years. Terrific guy. He's a lot of fun to be with. It is even said that he likes beautiful women as much as I do, and many of them are on the younger side." The 2003 fiftieth-birthday card to Epstein, quoted from the estate-released book: "Happy birthday — and may every day be another wonderful secret," signed beneath a hand-drawn outline of a naked woman in a typewritten imaginary dialogue — "We have certain things in common, Jeffrey"; "Enigmas never age. Have you noticed that?"; "A pal is a wonderful thing." The 2006 telephone call: when Palm Beach police arrested Epstein, Trump called the police chief, Michael Reiter, and said — in Goldman's account of the 302, "Thank goodness you're stopping him. Everyone has known he's been doing this." Goldman's gloss, delivered to the chamber, was a question rather than an assertion — the form a former prosecutor uses when he wants the jury to reach the conclusion itself: "Why would an innocent person call the police chief to report on an investigation after just learning about it?" A fair reading should hold the other possibility too, which Reiter's own 302 supports: that a man who believed he had seen nothing improper was warning a police chief about a man he had already thrown out of his club. This document holds both readings; the 302 is the evidence for each. The email from Epstein to Ghislaine Maxwell of April 2, 2011, released by House Democrats the previous November: "I want you to realize that that dog that hasn't barked is Trump. [Redacted victim] spent hours at my house with him. He has never once been mentioned." The flight records: "Trump flew on Epstein's private plane eight times, often with young women" — a figure drawn from the December 2023 released records showing eight flights in the 1990s. And a 2009 document redacted to the public but unredacted to Congress, recording answers Trump gave through his attorney to questions from Epstein's civil litigants: asked whether he had been to Epstein's house, "I may have been there with my wife." Asked whether young girls were there: "No. May have been children of guests, but that's it."

Twice during the five minutes, the Speaker pro tempore interrupted with the standing reminder that members are to "refrain from engaging in personalities toward the President" — the chamber's rule against direct attacks on a sitting president, invoked once when Goldman's language crossed the line and once at the close. The Record shows the speech's final words: "...EPSTEIN FILES. I yield back" [S-tier; the closing per the House live video].

The speech was, in the architecture of this document, a prosecutorial summation: every element argued, every exhibit cited by number, the opposing evidence acknowledged and discounted. What it was not — and this distinction runs through everything that follows — was a neutral presentation. Goldman is a partisan holding a case. The documents he read are real; the inferences he drew from them are his; and the viral post of October 18 converted both, in the compression of a share button, into "receipts." What the documents prove, what they allege, and what they cannot say is the work of the next ten Parts.

## Part II — The Accuser

### Chapter 3: Who the Documents Call Jane Doe 4

Begin with what is verifiable, because in this story the verifiable and the claimed have been blurred by everyone, for every reason.

The woman behind the interviews the press named "Jane Doe 4" met Jeffrey Epstein around 1984, on Hilton Head Island, South Carolina, when she was a young teenager. Her mother was a Hilton Head real-estate agent. Epstein, then in his early thirties and unknown to anyone in the town, rented a vacation home from the mother — the Post & Courier, which investigated her account more thoroughly than any other outlet, verified the rental, the mother's business, and the family's presence there in that period — and, the woman told the FBI, advertised for a babysitter through the mother's client materials. There were no children to babysit. On the first night, she told agents in 2019, Epstein offered her alcohol and drugs and raped her. This account matches, in its Hilton Head particulars, the lawsuit she filed against Epstein's estate in 2019 [A-tier: Greenville News, February 2026; WTOC; B-tier: ABC News].

The FBI interviewed her four times in 2019, under case file number 31E-NY-3027571 — the same federal case file that, in September 2019, would memorialize a separate cooperating witness's proffer interview on entirely different matters. Her identity is redacted in every released page. Media reporting describes her as a longtime Washington State resident, with fraud and theft charges there and a 2023 Georgia felony conviction for exploitation of an elderly person, and — as of mid-2026 — living "off the grid" at her family's urging, in fear of retaliation; her story became public the day after the Supreme Court rejected Trump's final appeal in the E. Jean Carroll case, a coincidence of news cycle noted by every outlet that covered her [A/B-tier: Guardian, June 2026; A-tier: Post & Courier].

She was represented in her estate claim by attorneys connected to Lisa Bloom's firm and later by Arick Fudali of Boies Schiller Flexner. She settled with the estate — and here the record carries one of this document's standing discrepancies, preserved rather than resolved: the Greenville News reported in February 2026 that "in 2021, she settled with the Epstein estate and voluntarily dismissed her claims," while Richard Kahn, one of the estate's executors, testified before Congress on March 11, 2026, that he believed she received a settlement through estate mediation in 2025 — a statement his attorney first affirmed and then, after an off-the-record discussion, formally withdrew to "we can neither confirm nor deny," prompting Representatives Garcia and Khanna to demand in writing whether she had filed a claim, whether the estate had paid her, and whether the estate knew of her allegations against Trump. No response is on the record. Separately, a victims' compensation program had deemed her ineligible — the reason is not public [T1: both dates documented; the discrepancy is flagged, not resolved].

What the Post & Courier did, and what every reader should know was done, is verify her biography against public records: the real-estate-agent mother, the mother's embezzlement conviction and federal prison sentence, the Ohio-linked associate she called "Atkins" — identified in records as Jimmy L. Atkins, who died in 2003 — and the peripheral details all checked. What the paper could not verify, and said so in print, is the allegation at the center of everything: that Donald Trump assaulted her. Roughly thirty to thirty-seven pages of her case documents remain unreleased in any form, a gap visible in the Bates-number sequence of the files themselves [A-tier: Post & Courier; NPR, March 5, 2026].

### Chapter 4: The Four Interviews, In Full

**The first contact** came days after Epstein's July 6, 2019 arrest, when a friend of the woman called the FBI's hotline on her behalf. A two-page intake form documenting that call was released on March 5, 2026, with the interview summaries [A-tier: NPR].

**Interview one** took place on July 24, 2019, at the FBI's field office. Its content was Epstein-only. She described the babysitting advertisement, the alcohol and drugs, the rape, and other men present on early occasions — she described them to the agent as "fat and disgusting," with Southern accents, who watched or masturbated — and she became emotional, and the interview was stopped. She provided the FBI a cropped photograph of Donald Trump standing with Epstein; her attorney would later contact the Southern District of New York about that photograph, which had been among the images on Epstein's phone [B-tier: ABC News].

**Interview two** — August 7, 2019, the interview Goldman read on the floor — was the first in which she described Trump. The account, as the interviewing agent memorialized it: Epstein transported her from South Carolina to New York or New Jersey when she was between thirteen and fifteen years old; she was introduced to Donald Trump inside "a very tall building with huge rooms"; Trump asked everyone else to leave the room; he said something to the effect of, "Let me teach you how little girls are supposed to be"; he unzipped his pants and put her head down toward his penis; she "bit the [expletive] out of it"; he struck her and said words to the effect of, "Get this little [expletive] the hell out of here." She told the agent she bit him because he disgusted her. Her recorded reason: "He had money, it reeked off of him." She also recalled his eye for her from the start — "from the get-go [he] didn't like that I was a boy-girl (tomboy)" — a small human detail in an otherwise terrible account. And the FBI's own summary of her file compressed the allegation to a single typed sentence that sat in the case file while the interviews sat outside the releases: "around 1983, when she was around 13 years old, Epstein introduced her to Trump, who subsequently forced her head down to his exposed penis which she subsequently bit. In response, Trump punched her in the head and kicked her out" [S-tier: 302 text via Snopes and the congressional record; T1: FBI summary].

The same interview ranged widely. She estimated her total interactions with Epstein at more than six but fewer than twenty, and said nearly every one involved abuse. She described the recruitment structure: Epstein sent her on "missions" to find young girls — "eat or be eaten," she explained, meaning that recruiting others reduced the abuse directed at her. She attributed to Epstein and Trump nicknames for the young women: "fresh meat," "untainted," "not jaded." She said she heard Trump and Epstein discuss blackmailing people, obtaining illegal building permits, and moving money through casinos, and she recorded an unexplained detail — "Number 6 is Donald Trump's favorite number." She described hearing an Epstein associate she called "Atkins" participate in the blackmail of her mother with explicit photographs, and an accountant named "Cecil" who helped the mother's books. She described the Rick James concert in Savannah when she was about fifteen: Epstein gave her alcohol, isolated her, and left; police found her and held her until her family retrieved her — Rick James is accused of nothing in any of this record. She described photographs given to the FBI: two undated photographs of herself at roughly thirteen to sixteen, and photographs of five girls at a party with Solo cups. And — the detail that matters most to the question of corroboration — she said there were two additional interactions with Trump beyond the one she described, and declined to detail them; when agents returned to the subject in a later session, she said, in the agent's paraphrase, "what's the point," after so many years [A/B-tier: released 302 text, EFTA02858481; Daily Beast, March 6-8, 2026].

**Interview three**, roughly two weeks after the second, refined the physical description: Trump, she said, "pulled her hair and punched her on the side of her head." She described the aftermath of the Trump incident: a blond woman, roughly Epstein's age, who offered her advice — "let me give you tip little girl about your breasts, wear a bra every night." She described the threats she said followed her through life: "numerous threat calls," beginning after the birth of her daughter, and four or five "close calls" in which SUVs ran her or her mother off the road in Oregon and Washington — attribution unverified in any document. She told the FBI she believed she was being followed, and she asked for protection [A/B-tier].

**Interview four** — October 16, 2019 — was the last. She had no attorney present. She was uncomfortable being recorded. The FBI asked her to return and specifically detail her contacts with Trump; she asked what the point would be, at this point in her life, and ended her cooperation. She was never called before a grand jury. No charges resulted. There is no public evidence the FBI followed up after the fourth interview; there is none that anyone contacted her attorney [A-tier: Guardian, June 30, 2026; Yahoo/Newsweek, March 5, 2026].

What the interview count itself means was put on the record by a Justice Department source speaking to the Miami Herald through the Daily Beast: "They would not have interviewed her four times if they thought she was lying" [A-tier]. The FBI's own later internal email supplies the institutional epitaph, in the cold shorthand of a case summary: "one identified victim claimed abuse by Trump but ultimately refused to cooperate" [T1: FBI email in released files]. Whether that refusal reflects a destroyed witness, a fabulist losing nerve, or a woman who calculated correctly that nothing would be done is not knowable from the record — and this document will not pretend otherwise.

### Chapter 5: The Counter-Evidence, In Full

Three threads cut against her, and a complete account gives each its full weight.

**The timeline problem.** Her allegation places the assault at approximately 1983-1984, when she was thirteen to fifteen. Every documented account of when Trump and Epstein met places the beginning of the friendship around 1985 at the earliest: Trump's own 2002 remark to New York Magazine — "I've known Jeff for fifteen years" — implies about 1987; the author Barry Levine dates their first meeting to 1987; a research archive of the files says "around 1985," when Trump bought Mar-a-Lago and Epstein was living in Palm Beach. Epstein's known Manhattan footprint fits no earlier introduction: the Wexner corporation bought the townhouse at 9 East 71st Street in August 1989 for $13.2 million, and Epstein did not move into it until 1995 or 1996, taking full possession in November 1998; before that, from 1992 to 1995, he leased 34 East 69th Street. The Guardian's careful formulation of March 6, 2026: "some of her statements appear to contradict what is known about Epstein's life in the early 1980s" [S-tier: property records; A-tier: the rest].

Her account has answers, and they deserve to be stated as her account's answers rather than as this document's conclusions. The venue was never identified beyond "a very tall building with huge rooms" in New York *or New Jersey* — and Trump Tower opened in 1983; an introduction in 1983-84 would require only that Epstein and Trump crossed paths earlier than the documentary record shows, which is possible precisely because the early record is thin. Levine's own timeline has been challenged in detail — his claim that Epstein was not in the townhouse until 1995 is consistent with the records, but his "1987 first meeting" rests on inference, not documents. The counter to the counter remains: her account says Epstein introduced them, and no document puts Epstein and Trump together before 1985. This stands as a live discrepancy — the single most important one in Part II — and it is logged as such, not resolved [T1/T2].

**The credibility record.** A Washington State criminal history — fraud and theft charges — and a 2023 Georgia felony for exploitation of an elderly person are part of the record. They do not disprove a crime committed against her at fourteen; the law's treatment of victims with imperfect lives is its own national scandal, documented in this record as in no other. But they were the White House's chosen frame when the allegations surfaced: "completely baseless accusations, backed by zero credible evidence, from a sadly disturbed woman who has an extensive criminal history," Karoline Leavitt said on March 6, 2026, adding the institutional defense — "Joe Biden's DOJ knew about them for four years and did nothing" — and the verdict: "President Trump has been totally exonerated by the release of the Epstein Files" [T1]. Each clause of that statement is checkable, and the checking is instructive: the interviews happened in 2019, under President Trump; the Department under President Biden did not act on them either; and the Trump Department released them in 2026 while calling them baseless. All three facts are true simultaneously. "Totally exonerated" is a political conclusion, not a legal one — no court has examined the allegation — and this document files it as such.

**The exculpatory file.** The same released corpus that contains her accusation contains material favorable to Trump, and Goldman read some of it into the record himself because it was part of his case that the denials fail. The fuller exculpatory inventory:

Palm Beach Police Chief Michael Reiter's 2019 FBI 302 records, from his own interview, that in 2006 Trump telephoned him: "Thank goodness you're stopping him. Everyone has known he's been doing this." Reiter's 302 says Trump told him he threw Epstein out of Mar-a-Lago; that "people in New York knew Epstein was disgusting"; that Trump called Maxwell "Epstein's operative... she is evil and to focus on her"; and that Trump "was around Epstein once when teenagers were present, and Trump got the hell out of there." Reiter's 302 also records that Trump "was one of the very first people to call when people found out they were investigating Epstein" — the fact pattern Goldman converted into an argument for consciousness of guilt and Reiter offered, on the face of the document, as assistance [A-tier].

Bradley Edwards — the victims' attorney who built the civil case against Epstein, later sued by Epstein and vindicated by Epstein's written apology, a man with no imaginable bias in Trump's favor — said on the record in 2019, standing beside Thomas Massie and Ro Khanna: in 2009, when he notified dozens of powerful people that he needed information, "Trump was the only person who picked up the phone and said: let's talk, I'll give you as much time as you need, I'll tell you what you need to know... was very helpful with the information he provided, gave no indication of involvement in anything improper, and had good information that checked out and helped us." So cooperative was Trump, Edwards said, that no subpoena was ever needed — a contrast Edwards himself drew with Clinton, who refused to cooperate, and with Wexner, who was not interviewed at all until Congress compelled him in 2026 [A-tier: Edwards press conference, September 2025].

Kahn testified he "never saw any type of transaction to Trump or anyone in his family." Kuvin, the first victims' attorney, reviewed security footage from Epstein's house and found no images of Trump in them. And the FBI's own boilerplate, printed at the top of the corpus the Department published, states what the law requires: a person's name appearing in the files "does not necessarily establish any wrongdoing" [T1].

The fair summary, and the only one this document will offer: a real woman, with a verified biography matching her account's checkable details and a documented terror of coming forward, gave a consistent core account four times to the FBI, which kept interviewing her and then lost her. The account was never tested in court, never corroborated by physical evidence, is contradicted on one checkable dimension — the timeline of the Trump-Epstein acquaintance — while matching on others, and sits in a corpus that also contains sworn and interview material favorable to the man she accuses. The FBI buried the file for years; that is proven, and Part III documents how. Whether her allegation is true is not knowable from the public record. Anyone who tells you otherwise — in either direction — is selling something.

### Chapter 6: The Other Accusers — A Complete Catalog

Jane Doe 4 is not the only woman to have accused Donald Trump within the Epstein record, and each of the others must be documented with the same discipline — including the one whose case collapsed into a documented hoax. The catalog follows, in the order each thread entered the record.

**"Katie Johnson" and the 2016 lawsuits.** On April 26, 2016 — in the middle of the Republican primary — a pro se complaint was filed in federal court in California, Case 5:16-cv-00797, by a "Katie Johnson" against Donald Trump and Jeffrey Epstein. The cover sheet is preserved: a demand of $100,000,000, theories under 18 U.S.C. and 42 U.S.C. §1985, two causes of action — sexual abuse under threat of harm, and conspiracy to deprive civil rights. The claim: that in the summer of 1994, at age thirteen, she had been lured from her home by a recruiter named "Tiffany," transported to New York, and subjected to four encounters with Trump at parties at Epstein's residence, including forcible rape; and that both men threatened her and her family with death if she spoke [T1: docket].

The California case was dismissed in May 2016 — defective service, a bad address, no valid federal claim. It was refiled in the Southern District of New York in June (1:16-cv-04642), voluntarily dismissed in September, and refiled again with attorneys in late September (1:16-cv-07673). The September 30 amended complaint carried two supporting declarations. "Tiffany Doe" declared she had worked for Epstein from 1991 to 2000 as a "party planner" whose occupation was recruiting adolescent women; that the parties were "mostly held at what is known as the Wexner Mansion located at 9 E. 71st St."; that she personally witnessed four sexual encounters between the plaintiff and Trump, the fourth being forcible rape "despite her pleas to stop"; that she witnessed an occasion on which "Mr. Trump forced the Plaintiff and a 12-year-old female named Maria perform oral sex on Mr. Trump and witnessed his physical abuse of both minors when they finished the act"; and that she witnessed Trump tell the plaintiff "she shouldn't ever say anything if she didn't want to disappear like the 12-year-old female Maria... capable of having her whole family killed." "Joan Doe," a schoolmate, corroborated confidences from the 1994-95 school year [T1: declarations, Case 1:16-cv-07673, Documents 1-1 and 4].

On November 2, 2016 — four days before the presidential election — a press conference was scheduled at the offices of attorney Lisa Bloom, who had taken on the representation. It was canceled: the plaintiff, who was by then in hiding, had received threats; Bloom's website was hacked by someone claiming affiliation with Anonymous; and the plaintiff stopped responding. On November 4, two days before the vote, the case was voluntarily dismissed. It has never been refiled [A-tier: Bloom's account in her book and interviews; B-tier].

Then the case unraveled on its own promoters. The name was an alias: the FBI's own complaint form, FD-71, dated November 23, 2016, records her attorney telling the Bureau that "Tiffany instructed Doe to use the name Katie Johnson at these parties" [T1: EFTA00129127]. The two men who had promoted the litigation — shopping a disguised "witness" video for $1 million — included Norm Lubow, a former Jerry Springer show producer with a documented history of fabricated stories, whom Snopes confirmed as a central promoter, and Steve Baer, a Never-Trump activist. The one journalist who spoke with the plaintiff by phone, Emily Shugerman of the (now-defunct) Revelist, wrote that she hung up "questioning whether Johnson really existed." Retired Detective Mike Fisten, who had investigated Epstein for years, said Epstein "never had parties like described." And Lisa Bloom — who is no friend of Trump's and whom no evidence connects to any Trump coordination — wrote in her 2021 book that her client instructed her to drop the case, that "Trump's people never reached out," that there was "absolutely no communication with anyone in the Trump team," and that she has not spoken to the woman since 2016 [B-tier; Bloom's book "Perversion of Justice," 2021]. A separate thread connects the case to Trump's former fixer: Michael Cohen, under oath, admitted threatening five hundred or more people on Trump's behalf in 2016 — the same year the Johnson suit vanished four days before the election — and his later "empty Bronx lot, a baby, and someone named Mary Jane Doe" story does not match any filed Katie Johnson complaint, a mismatch documented rather than explained [T1/T2].

The thread matters for two reasons beyond its collapse. First, the primary documents are real filings with real docket numbers, and their specific content — the Wexner Mansion venue, the Maria threat — recurs in other threads of this record. Second, "Katie Johnson" and "Jane Doe 4" are frequently conflated online, and the record formally separates them: Johnson's account places the events in New York City in the summer of 1994 with a Port Authority recruitment; Jane Doe 4's account places them in the Hilton Head orbit around 1983-84 with a babysitting-advertisement recruitment. Different women, different decades, different stories. Both remain in this document, each at its own tier [T1].

**The "good one" girl.** The FBI's 302s record a second, separate accuser whose account was summarized in the 21-page presentation: a woman who said Epstein introduced her to Trump when she was fourteen, saying "This is a good one, right," and that both men smiled, nodded, and chuckled. Her account also appears in the NTOC files with the detail that the introduction happened in a dark green car, and that Trump "got the best of me that day." She is not further identified in the public record. She was, per the FBI's email, one of the accusers who "ultimately refused to cooperate" [B-tier: NY Mag; T1: FBI email].

**The NTOC tip cluster.** The National Threat Operations Center's intake files — raw tips from the public, logged and largely never investigated to conclusion — contain a catalog of Trump-related claims, each graded in the record itself: a 1984 Lake Michigan allegation, in which a caller said she was trafficked at thirteen, gave birth, and that her uncle killed the child and threw it in Lake Michigan "while Trump watched" — a claim Snopes found timeline-impossible and wholly uncorroborated; a 1995-96 Rancho Palos Verdes golf-course claim involving alleged sex parties with Robin Leach, which the FBI's own report graded not credible; "orgy party" claims involving Victoria's Secret models and, in one version, Bill Clinton; a Mar-a-Lago claim alleging rape at thirteen at parties and a "finger-rating" auction of girls attended by, among others, members of the Trump family and Alan Dershowitz; a September 2012 email from an Epstein associate proposing a Mar-a-Lago Christmas visit "instead of his island"; and a 302 summary recording a tip that "a drugged woman believed Trump raped her and then vanished forever." One file, EFTA01660679, memorializes the last of these. Each is documented as a tip; none advanced to a charge; the FBI's own credibility annotations are part of the record [T1: released files].

**Stacey Williams.** In the released files, Williams — a former model — said Epstein took her to Trump Tower in 1993, where Trump groped her; a postcard to her later bore the handwritten note "Stacey — Your home away from home. Love Donald." The Trump campaign called her account "unequivocally false" [B-tier].

**The limousine driver.** Among the material Representative Lieu showed Attorney General Bondi at the February hearing was a 1995 statement by a limousine driver who said he overheard Trump on a telephone call with "Jeffrey" about "abusing some girl," and who said he knew an alleged victim who "had her head blown off" — a death police declined to call a suicide. The Department never interviewed him, per Lieu's account [B-tier: Salon, February 12, 2026].

**Elyse Relford.** On October 3, 2026, a Kentucky woman named Elyse Relford filed a pro se complaint in Palm Beach County court, Case 50-2026-CA-011136-XXXA-MB, alleging a 1997 Florida hotel rape with Epstein present, demanding $1.3 million, and claiming among other things that Trump had impersonated her biological father, from whom Epstein separated her. The complaint was placed on administrative hold for deficiencies; it names neither the Department nor any Trump entity as a party. It is the fourth public allegation thread of 2026, and it joins the catalog as the others did: documented, unadjudicated, tier-flagged [B-tier].

A decade earlier and one club over, a parallel account already existed: "Jane Doe 102," a federal filing by a young woman who said she was fifteen in 1998, working at Mar-a-Lago while her father maintained the grounds, when Maxwell recruited her with the massage-and-money pitch; she alleged hidden cameras, escape to Australia, and later contact from Epstein's people. Her filing named Trump only as a possible witness — "might have relevant information" — a distinction between the filings that this document preserves because the record does [T1].

The catalog's shape deserves one observation before the chapter closes. The accusations of Trump in the released record are numerous, and their provenance is heterogeneous: four FBI interviews of one woman; a second accuser in the 302s; a cluster of NTOC tips the FBI itself graded; a 2016 lawsuit whose promoters were frauds; a driver whose statement was never investigated; a 2026 pro se filing. Grouping them as "the receipts" — or dismissing them as "the hoax" — is equally false to the record. Each item is what it is, and this document has tried to keep each item exactly that.

### Chapter 7: What the FBI Made of It — the 21-Page Presentation

In mid-July 2025, as public pressure mounted on the Department to explain its July 7 "no client list, case closed" memo, an FBI–NYPD task force bearing the Child Exploitation and Human Trafficking Task Force logo assembled a slide presentation for leadership. A version reached FBI Director Kash Patel on August 7, 2025. The assignment email survives, dated July 24, 2025: "Can you write me a sentence or two for the below — these are the salacious statements made against the individuals in the file." Summaries of thirteen people were drafted within two hours [S-tier: EFTA01648951 and related].

The deck, when released in January 2026 as EFTA01660639, contained a timeline of the Epstein investigations, Epstein-Maxwell financial transactions, a proffer-session attendee list, and a "Prominent Names" slide listing about a dozen men: Donald Trump, Bill Clinton, Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor, Harvey Weinstein, Leon Black, Les Wexner, Jes Staley, Glenn Dubin, Alan Dershowitz, William Barr, and Jean-Luc Brunel. Against Trump it carried two entries: Jane Doe 4's account, and the "good one, right" introduction. A July 22, 2025 email in the files marked Trump a "positive case hit" while noting that one identified accuser "claimed abuse by Trump but ultimately refused to cooperate." The deck's statistics: more than ninety identified victims, over two hundred ninety tips received, fifty-two anonymous, two hundred thirty-nine followed up, twenty-seven secondary contacts [A/B-tier: Miami Herald, February 7, 2026; NY Mag].

A NewsNation investigation added a layer the slides did not: according to its reporting, the presentation grew out of the case of an Epstein survivor who served as the FBI's primary confidential informant on the ring — an adult at the time of her abuse — who told the Bureau she was directed to give massages, "was put in compromising positions and was a victim of sexual abuse," and who alleged Epstein trafficked her to Harvey Weinstein (who, she said, appeared at her door and tried to follow her into the shower after a job offer) and to Leon Black (whom she accused of raping her repeatedly, with threats). Dershowitz responded by naming himself as a target of her claims — "I never received a massage on Epstein's plane... She made up the entire story. In doing so, she violated section 1001 of the criminal code — lying to the FBI... I'm planning to sue her as 'Jane Doe' and subpoenaing the Justice Department and FBI to reveal the name of this felon." A member of Congress who has reviewed the unredacted files told NewsNation the informant "was allegedly trafficked to several of Epstein's friends." Her identity remains redacted; the discrepancy between Dershowitz's "she was not a minor, she is a criminal" and the Bureau's protective handling of her is unresolved [B-tier: NewsNation, February 2026; flagged].

And then the Bureau's bottom line, published in the January 2026 release and never retracted: the FBI's file review concluded there was "ample proof that Epstein sexually abused underage girls" but "scant evidence" of a documented trafficking operation to clients — no client list, in the evidentiary sense. That finding is itself contested within this record: the witness-tampering wires, the recruiter payments, the message pads, and the flight logs all cut against it, and each is documented in its proper place below. But it is the finding, and it drove everything that followed — including, ironically, both the Department's insistence that there was nothing more to release and the public's refusal to believe it [A-tier].

## Part III — The Withholding Story

### Chapter 8: The Law Passed Over the President's Objection

To understand the releases, one must understand the statute, because everything the Justice Department did for eleven months after November 19, 2025 is measured against its text — and everything a federal judge did after April 2026 is measured against it too.

The Act's passage was, in the language of Congress watchers, a discharge-petition earthquake. Through the summer and fall of 2025, House leadership refused to bring an Epstein-release bill to the floor. So Representatives Thomas Massie, a Kentucky Republican, and Ro Khanna, a California Democrat, did what the rules allow and the leadership rarely suffers: they filed a discharge petition, which forces a vote if a majority of the House — 218 members — signs it. The President told Republicans that signing it would be "a hostile act" — in Representative Jeffries' on-record formulation — a characterization confirmed by multiple members. Representative Melanie Stansbury said on the floor, weeks before the vote, what members were hearing in the hallways: "The United States president is threatening members of his own party in this chamber from signing a petition that would release the full unredacted Epstein files. So my question is, what are they hiding? Who are they protecting?" The petition gathered 218 commitments anyway — including, in the end, four Republicans [T1: Stansbury floor remarks, September 3, 2025; petition records, October 8, 2026 status: 216 signatures with commitments for 218].

The floor debate of November 18, 2025, preserved in the Record, is worth a paragraph of its own, because it shows what the Act meant to the members who forced it. Massie: "I'm embarrassed by my own party." Marjorie Taylor Greene — who would quit Congress three days later, in a rupture with the President in which the Epstein fight figured prominently — wore white, the suffrage color, and said: "I remain dedicated to my purpose to read those names here on the House floor if those women ever find themselves in a place where it needs to be done." Mace, Boebert, Raskin, Jordan, McGovern, Kamlager-Dove all spoke. Raskin told Massie: "I have never heard a more defensive presentation... spend less time pointing out hypocrisy and more time trying to ferret out cruelty, humiliation, and rape." The House passed the bill 427 to 1. The floor debate's texture, preserved in the Record: Speaker Johnson had shut down the Rules Committee and sent members home early in July to avoid amendment votes, per McGovern's on-record account; Massie counted a "49-day" stall engineered by refusing to swear in a new member; Knott worried aloud about the "bellman, waiter, doorman" whose names might surface — the amendment's intent, he was assured, was otherwise; and Plaskett, in the censure debate interwoven with the bill's, said: "I remain dedicated to my purpose to read those names here on the House floor if those women ever find themselves in a place where it needs to be done." The Senate passed it by unanimous consent. On November 19, 2025, the President signed it, one day after posting "THE JEFFREY EPSTEIN HOAX" and directing that Epstein's relationships with "Bill Clinton, Larry Summers, Reid Hoffman and JPMorgan" be investigated — a directive, Senator Whitehouse would later note, naming no Republicans [S-tier: Congressional Record; T1: Trump's Nov 14, 2025 Truth Social post].

The statute's operative text drives everything after. Section 2(a) enumerated nine categories to be released: the Epstein and Maxwell cases including all investigations, prosecutions, and custodial records; flight logs and travel records including manifests, itineraries, pilot records, and customs and immigration records for any aircraft, vessel, or vehicle; individuals, including government officials, named or referenced in connection with criminal activity, civil settlements, immunity agreements, or investigative material; entities with known or alleged ties; immunity deals, non-prosecution agreements, plea bargains, and sealed settlements; internal Justice Department communications about decisions to charge, not charge, investigate, or decline; all communications concerning the destruction, deletion, alteration, misplacement, or concealment of documents; and documentation of Epstein's detention and death, including incident reports, witness interviews, medical examiner files, autopsy reports, and written records of the circumstances and cause of death. Section 2(b) then walled off the escape routes, in language a judge would later quote back to the government: "no record shall be withheld, delayed or redacted on the basis of embarrassment, reputational harm, or political sensitivity, including to any government or public figure, or foreign dignitary." Section 2(c) permitted withholding on exactly five grounds: victims' personally identifiable information and personal or medical files, child sexual-abuse imagery, materials in active federal investigations or prosecutions (narrowly tailored and temporary), images of death or abuse or injury, and properly classified information — each requiring a written justification published in the Federal Register, each post-July-2025 classification decision required to be published with the classifying authority named and an unclassified summary provided, and everything classified required to be declassified to the maximum extent possible. Section 3 required a report to Congress within fifteen days of the release's completion: categories released and withheld, a redaction summary with legal bases, and a list of every government official and politically exposed person named or referenced — a list on which no redactions were permitted under 2(b). The release deadline was thirty days [S-tier: Public Law 119-38, text quoted in Phang v. Blanche, Doc 16].

The deadline was missed. The next nine chapters document the year that followed, in the order it unfolded.

### Chapter 9: Phase One — "The Client List Is on My Desk"

The administration's Epstein policy began, as these things do, with a promise. In February 2025, weeks into office, Attorney General Pam Bondi told Fox News that the Epstein client list was "sitting on my desk right now to review." It was not. There was no client list in the sense she implied, a fact the White House chief of staff, Susie Wiles, would eventually concede on January 1, 2026 — "there is no client list" — while acknowledging the political operation had misjudged how much the President's own supporters cared [T1: Bondi, Fox, February 2025; Wiles via Times of India, January 1, 2026].

On February 27, 2025, the Department released "Phase 1" — roughly three hundred pages of mostly already-public material, handed to select conservative influencers at the White House in binders stamped with the Department seal. The gesture backfired; the base noticed the thinness. Then, on July 7, 2025, the Department and the FBI issued their memorandum: an "exhaustive review," no client list, nothing warranting further investigation, no more releases. The memo was signed off at the highest levels of both institutions; within days, reporting revealed that Bondi and Blanche had personally told the President he was in the files, and that the memo's no-more-releases conclusion had been reached as the Department quietly pursued a grand-jury unsealing in the Maxwell case — a maneuver Representative Goldman called, in real time, an "end-run around the Southern District" [A-tier: NY Mag timeline; WSJ, May 2025; Goldman statement, July 22, 2025].

The memo detonated the President's political base. On July 16, 2025, he attacked his own supporters as "stupid" and "foolish" for asking about Epstein. On July 29, 2025, aboard Air Force One, he offered his fullest account of the falling-out: Epstein, he said, had "stolen" young women, including Virginia Giuffre, from the Mar-a-Lago spa — asked whether Giuffre was among those stolen, "I think so. He stole her." Elon Musk, whose feud with the President was then at its height, had posted on June 5 that the administration was withholding the documents because Trump was in them: "The truth will come out." FBI Director Kash Patel and Deputy Director Dan Bongino took to Fox to insist Epstein's death was suicide — Bongino: "I have seen the whole file. He killed himself" — and the Bongino-Bondi feud over the handling became public. By November, the House had passed the Act over all of it [A-tier].

### Chapter 10: Phase Two — the Releases and Their Failures

**December 19, 2025.** The first statutory batch arrived on the deadline day the Department had missed: a fraction of the material, thin on new information, Trump's name "virtually non-existent" in the New York Times' count of the day's production. The same day, the Department's letter to Congress invoked privileges it did not detail and promised a privilege log "in due course" — a log that, as of this writing, has never been published in full. Thousands of files posted that day were pulled from the website within twenty-four hours [S/T1: NPR, December 19, 2025; DOJ letter].

**January 29-30, 2026.** The "final" release: some 3.5 million pages in total, per the Department, including roughly two thousand videos and one hundred eighty thousand images. It contained the 21-page presentation and Jane Doe 4's allegations. It arrived with a preemptive statement no one had asked for, asserting that the files could contain "fake or falsely submitted" documents and "untrue and sensationalist claims against President Trump," which — the statement argued — "if they have a shred of credibility, they certainly would have been weaponized against President Trump already." The statement was the Department grading its own evidence before anyone else could [T1: DOJ statement, January 30, 2026].

**The discovery of the gaps.** Within days, independent journalists found what the release was missing. Roger Sollenberger, working independently, reported that witness interviews were absent — the White House press secretary's own acknowledgment that an "FBI interview" existed had set him looking. NPR confirmed missing 302s on February 24. CNN found that dozens of FBI witness interviews were missing from the public database, including three concerning the Trump accuser. The precise arithmetic: NPR counted fifty-three missing pages; ABC identified a Bates gap of more than fifty pages between EFTA00057715 and EFTA00057769; the count resolved, after the March releases, to thirty-seven pages still outstanding. House Democrats who had reviewed unredacted files at the Department's offices confirmed the 302s existed and had been withheld; the ranking member, Robert Garcia, opened a parallel investigation and was told by the Department that his claims were "misleading the public while manufacturing outrage" [S/A-tier: NPR; CNN; ABC; Politico, March 5, 2026].

**March 5-6, 2026.** Under subpoena pressure — the committee had voted 24-19, with Republican votes, to subpoena the Attorney General — the Department posted the missing material: sixteen pages of interview summaries plus the two-page intake form, released as part of a larger tranche. The explanation, delivered by the Department's official account, became the year's most quoted sentence about the files: the records had been "incorrectly coded as duplicates." Fifteen documents were affected. No individual was ever named as responsible; the Department's inspector general later opened an audit whose scope includes exactly this question. Representative Khanna's further claim — that the FBI had "scrubbed" the files in March 2025, long before the Act — was reported by the BBC as unsubstantiated, and it is logged here as an allegation, not a fact [A-tier: Reuters, March 6; BBC, February 10].

**The redaction failures** were, if anything, worse than the omissions. The Wall Street Journal's review of the releases found at least forty-three victims' full names exposed, more than twenty-four of them minors at the time of their abuse, some names printed more than a hundred times, with home addresses keyword-searchable in the files. Unredacted nude images of women and girls were published and removed only after journalists asked. One document appeared twice in the release — once with Trump's name redacted, once without — the older version going viral. A victim of Jeffrey Epstein whom the files identified in a 2021 victim-notification email as covered by the Florida non-prosecution agreement's confidentiality promise found her name exposed throughout. One document appeared twice in the same release — once with Trump's name redacted, once without — the older version going viral before anyone explained which was true. And when members were finally allowed to check the work, Representative Raskin ran "Trump," "Donald," and "Don" through the Department's own reading-room database tool and received more than a million results — with the caveat, on the record, that "I obviously didn't have the time to review each one... I cannot guarantee that every Donald is Donald Trump as opposed to some other Donald," the tool itself "confusing, unreliable, and clunky" [S/A-tier]. The FBI's own February 2, 2026 letter to the judges overseeing the Maxwell docket admitted "several thousands of documents and media that may have inadvertently included victim-identifying information," disclosed and then removed; victims' counsel told the court the harm had "escalated from concern to suffering and fear for personal safety... irreversible." Representative Garcia would state publicly that the Department "took down 50k files with no explanation"; the Department said tens of thousands were temporarily removed to address victim exposures. And the forensic finding that may outlast all of it: in at least two documents, new black-bar redactions had been painted over previously released text — in one case stripping the names of the entire 2006-2007 federal prosecution team (Jeffrey Sloman, Matthew Menchel, Andy Lourie, Ann Marie Villafaña, Dexter Lee, Bruce Reinhart, and the state prosecutors) from a document that had already been public, a finding verified by side-by-side image comparison [S/A-tier: WSJ; CNN; github forensic comparison, EFTA00205380 and EFTA00179797].

**The aftermath for the victims.** Reuters reported in June 2026 that survivors were being harassed after the releases — homes photographed, strange cars outside, threats including callers claiming to know addresses; some no longer left home alone. The Department's spokesperson said no victim should face harassment and that the Department was "not to blame for backlash directed at victims who voluntarily revealed their identities long before files were published" — a sentence the survivors' lawyers disputed fact by fact [A-tier: Reuters, June 10].

### Chapter 11: The Reading Room

On February 9, 2026, the Department opened its files to members of Congress under conditions that would become their own scandal: four computers, unique logins, staff seated directly behind the reviewing member, DOJ-issued notepads only, no phones, no personal staff, twenty-four hours' notice. Representatives Khanna and Massie spent roughly two hours reviewing what was billed as the unredacted set and found 70 to 80 percent of it still redacted. The session produced two disclosures that traveled the world.

The first: the "torture video" email. An April 24, 2009 exchange in the files showed Epstein writing to a redacted recipient: "where are you? are you ok, I loved the torture video," and the reply the next day: "I am in china I will be in the US 2nd week of may." Massie named the redacted party on the House floor the next day: Sultan Ahmed bin Sulayem, the Dubai ports magnate. Deputy Attorney General Blanche responded publicly that the redaction protected an email address, that "the Sultan's name is available unredacted in the files. See EFTA00666117. Be honest, and stop grandstanding" — and Khanna named six men whose names had been redacted on the theory that they were victims, among them bin Sulayem, Les Wexner, and the Italian politician Nicola Caputo, reading them under the protection of the Speech or Debate Clause: "Donald Trump's FBI scrubbed these files in March," he said — the claim the BBC could not substantiate — "long before... the Epstein Transparency Act. That means the survivors' statements to the FBI naming rich and powerful men who went to Epstein's island — they're all hidden." Bin Sulayem resigned as chairman and CEO of DP World on February 13, 2026 [S/A-tier: Newsweek; CBS; Reuters].

The second: the surveillance. Two days later, at the House Judiciary Committee, a photographer captured a document in the Attorney General's hearing binder titled "Jayapal Pramila Search History" — a printout of a member of Congress's searches in that reading room, with file numbers and descriptions, in order. Representative Jayapal confirmed its accuracy — "my search history exactly in the order that I searched it" — and told CNN she learned of the tracking from the photograph, not from the Department. Representative Mace confirmed separately that the Department was "TAGGING ALL DOCUMENTS" with timestamps. Speaker Johnson called it "inappropriate... I'm sure it was an oversight"; Raskin demanded an inspector-general investigation; the three members demanded in writing that the tracking cease. The Department said it "logs all searches made on its systems to protect against the release of victim information" and never explained why the log sat in the Attorney General's binder [S-tier: AP; NPR; CNN, February 11-13].

### Chapter 12: The Bondi Arc, In Full

Pam Bondi's Epstein chapter opened with the client-list promise and closed, nineteen months later, with a thyroid-cancer disclosure and a seat on the White House AI panel. In between, on February 11, 2026, came the hearing that anchored everything.

**February 11, 2026 — House Judiciary.** Bondi, under oath, faced the committee for the Department's annual oversight. The exchange that made history came when Representative Lieu asked about women claiming encounters with Trump through Epstein: "This is so ridiculous," Bondi answered. "There is no evidence that Donald Trump has committed a crime. Everyone knows that." Lieu: "I believe you just lied under oath." Bondi, leaning forward: "Don't you ever accuse me of committing a crime." The same hearing contained her clash with Raskin — she called him a "washed-up, loser lawyer"; he asked about co-conspirator indictments and she answered by observing that "the DOW is over $50,000"; survivors in the audience stood at Jayapal's request; Bondi declined to apologize for the exposure of victims' names — "I'm not going to get in the gutter for her theatrics" — and accused Representative Balint of antisemitism, prompting Balint to walk out. Representative Correa elicited a promise: "If any man's name was redacted, that should not have been. We will, of course, unredact it" [S-tier: hearing transcript; Axios; Salon].

**The perjury demand.** On February 25, 2026, Representatives Lieu and Goldman formally demanded that the Deputy Attorney General appoint a special counsel to investigate Bondi for perjury. On March 17, 2026, Representative Summer Lee introduced five articles of impeachment — defiance of the Oversight subpoena, violation of the EFTA, abuse of investigatory power for political retaliation and for shielding the President and his allies, defiance of federal court orders, and perjury in congressional testimony — drafted with the advocacy group Free Speech For People. The articles were referred to the Judiciary Committee and died there, as parallel impeachment articles against other officials also died in the 119th Congress [T1: H.Res.1119].

**The firing.** On April 2, 2026, the President fired her, announcing it on Truth Social; she was informed in the Oval Office the day before, ahead of an Iran address. The official explanation was a private-sector future; the reporting — CBS News, attributed to sources — was that the President had concluded she was a political liability on the files and had failed to prosecute his enemies, privately calling her a "weak and ineffective enforcer." Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, the President's former personal defense counsel in the hush-money and documents cases, became Acting Attorney General; Lee Zeldin was floated; Blanche was nominated on June 8 and confirmed by a narrow overnight Senate vote in August, sworn in by Judge Emil Bove at the White House — an appointment that carried its own warning labels, catalogued at the time: more than twelve hundred former Justice Department employees opposed his nomination, and a hundred-and-one-judge ethics complaint followed his confirmation. And the reporting on Bondi's fall attributed it in part to her failure to prosecute the President's enemies — the Comey and James indictments dismissed, the Powell probe quashed, the Schiff and Swalwell matters stalled [S/A-tier].

**The deposition.** The committee had subpoenaed Bondi on March 4, 2026; the Department responded on April 8, after her firing, with the argument that her departure rendered the subpoena moot — a position Democrats called absurd, moving civil contempt. A deal was struck for a voluntary, transcribed, unsworn, unrecorded interview, which took place on May 29, 2026, in Room 2154 of the Rayburn House Office Building, with survivors calling "tell the truth" as she entered, escorted by Department officials including Civil Rights Division head Harmeet Dhillon, who acted as her counsel — a conflict Democrats protested. The 111-page transcript, released June 4, contains the following load-bearing passages. Her opening line: "justice and transparency in this matter have been delivered at the direction of President Trump and his administration." On errors: "There were redaction errors" — three categories she named: victims' names published, nude images posted, and the names of potential co-conspirators redacted when they should not have been. On Trump's name and the redaction process, asked repeatedly whether it came up: "I don't recall." On delegation: she had delegated oversight to Blanche — "Todd Blanche is one of the most highly ethical individuals I know... he managed this investigation — and it was a Herculean task — with very little error" — while steering the Maxwell-transcript redaction decisions his way. ## Part IV — The Courtroom: Phang v. Blanche, In Full

### Chapter 14: The Plaintiff and the Judge

Katie Phang is a lawyer and independent journalist, formerly a cable-news host, based in Miami, who had made seventy-two videos about the Epstein files and built an audience of millions on the subject. On April 27, 2026, she sued Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia, Case 1:26-cv-01417. The theory mattered: the government insisted the EFTA created no private right of action, and the Department would spend the next six months saying so. Phang's answer was that she did not need one. She sued under the Administrative Procedure Act, which permits any person suffering legal wrong from agency action to challenge it as "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law," and under the Declaratory Judgment Act. Her filing specifically identified the withheld categories and, within them, the documents referring to President Trump. She was represented by Brendan Ballou, Samuel Ward-Packard, and Emily Gilman of the Public Integrity Project; the government's team would rotate from Saifuddin Kalolwala (who withdrew September 1) to Stanley Woodward Jr. to Andrew Block [S-tier: complaint; docket].

The case drew Judge Emmet G. Sullivan, and the draw was its own kind of destiny. Sullivan was appointed to the federal district court by President Clinton on June 15, 1994 — after earlier appointments to the D.C. Superior Court by President Reagan in 1984 and to the D.C. Court of Appeals by President George H.W. Bush in 1991, making him the first person in the capital's history appointed to three judicial positions by three presidents. Born June 4, 1947, a Howard University graduate, he took senior status in 2021. And he carried a history with the Justice Department that colleagues said left a settled mark: as the trial judge in Senator Ted Stevens's 2008 corruption case, he held three federal prosecutors in contempt over undisclosed evidence, watched the conviction collapse when an FBI whistleblower revealed more than thirty withheld documents, and purged the prosecutors from the case. Colleagues who watched it told reporters he came out of the Stevens saga with a particular view of what happens when the Department decides the rules do not apply to it — a view he would spend the summer of 2026 communicating to the Department's lawyers at unusual length [S-tier: Congressional Directory; Courthouse News, August 13].

### Chapter 15: The Preliminary Injunction — June 25, 2026

On June 25, 2026, Sullivan granted Phang a preliminary injunction in a forty-eight-page opinion that is now the controlling judicial reading of the Act. Its reasoning, in sequence.

**Standing.** Phang had it twice over. Informational injury: the Act's disclosure mandate confers a concrete interest, and "half a dozen stories she is currently unable to report" is particular to her — the opinion citing the line of authority from Akins through Campaign Legal Center and Public Citizen v. Carlin. Economic injury: lost video revenue, the "classic form of concrete and particularized harm," redressable by removing the hurdle. FOIA would not substitute: the Act's mandate is "strikingly broad" — quoting United States v. Maxwell, 811 F. Supp. 3d 667, 676 (S.D.N.Y. 2025) — and the Department's own EFTA-FOIA "Cross Walk" concedes the Act "requires disclosures that would be exempt under FOIA." The government had argued, among other things, that Phang was an improper plaintiff because the Act served the public; the opinion answered that a public entitlement does not dissolve an individual's particular stake in it [S-tier: opinion, Doc 16].

**The violation.** The opinion then walked the compliance record: the December 19 letter invoking privilege with the promised log never produced; the December 20, 22, 23 and January 30 productions arriving with no privilege log at all; the January 30 letter claiming completion while reporting roughly two hundred thousand pages redacted or withheld on common-law privileges that appear nowhere in the statute's five categories; the foreign-language materials never reviewed ("not practicable for a first-level reviewer," the Department said — to which the opinion's later order responds that administrative inconvenience excuses nothing); the redaction log the Act required by December 19, 2025. And the sentence that became the headline, appearing in the stay-denial section: "The Attorney General has conceded that he is in violation of the Act." The Act, the opinion observed, "required the Attorney General to produce the documents and publish the log by December 19, 2025 — more than six months ago." The June 25 order granted the injunction, required production or justification on the specified categories within days, and denied the government's requested stay — both the seven-day and sixty-day versions [S-tier].

**The five categories** the injunction addressed, with their document numbers, because the numbers are the map of what the fight is about: (1) eight email exchanges concerning the "torture video" correspondence of April 2009 and related sexual-activity emails with young women including minors — EFTA00749245, EFTA01187999, EFTA01930501, EFTA01928255, EFTA00628112, EFTA02648868, EFTA02504630, EFTA01022356; (2) a draft indictment's co-conspirator redactions and the senders and recipients of briefing emails — EFTA01703108, EFTA00038227; (3) the handwritten FBI interview notes underlying the FD-302s — EFTA01245620, EFTA02858481, EFTA02858491, EFTA02858495 — the notes, the opinion notes, of "a victim who has alleged that in the 1980s, when she was about 13 years old, Epstein introduced her to Trump, who in turn assaulted her"; (4) foreign-language materials never reviewed or produced; and (5) the redaction log never published. Relief: a declaratory judgment that the withholding violated the Act, an injunction requiring production or documented justification, and — in the complaint's prayer — a special master to oversee compliance. A one-dollar nominal bond was set, the authority cited being Beatty v. Trump [S-tier].

**Irreparable harm**, in the passage that most startled the lawyers who read it: "the current high level of interest in the Epstein Files combined with the upcoming mid-term elections amounts to a circumstance that itself constitutes irreparable harm, especially where the Attorney General has not disputed that he is in violation of the Epstein Act." Elections, the court held, are not a reason to withhold — they are a reason to hurry [S-tier].

The Department's public response came within hours and set the tone for the summer. A spokesperson: "Judge Sullivan's perverse interpretation appears to be focused on driving misleading headlines. This judge is suggesting DOJ violate the law by un-redacting victim names, who as the Department has always explained, sadly became co-conspirators. DOJ has produced all responsive documents and will appeal this decision with confidence." Phang's counsel answered: "The government thought that it could ignore its own law and blow off a judge's order." Phang herself, on her platform: "the Trump regime does not get the final word on what the public can see. The judge does" [T1].

### Chapter 16: The Summer of Compliance — July Through October

What followed was a procedural war whose every filing is public, and whose chronology matters because it shows a Department complying only where compliance could not be deferred.

**July 2.** The Department missed the OSC deadline; the court gave five days and ordered in-camera review. The Department's declarant, Assistant Attorney General Stanley Woodward, explained the redactions: the withheld names were "victims or potential victims whose counsel have contacted the government and requested that the persons be treated as victims" — an assertion the court rejected in one sentence ("The Defendant's assertions do not comply with the Court's Order, which requires documentation to support the assertions"), and in the order that became the summer's anthem: "'Victims' is the word Congress used. Potential victims does not appear in that provision anywhere."; the handwritten notes were "deemed duplicative of the typewritten reports"; handwriting "complicates the redaction process... technical limitations on the Department's ability to run meaningful quality control checks for victim PII across handwritten materials"; and the Department could not locate an unredacted version of the 2007 draft-indictment photocopy because the redactions pre-existed in its own copy [S/T1].

**July 25-30.** The court ordered the in-camera submission; the Department hand-delivered ten unredacted documents to the judge's chambers on September 24... no — the July 25 order directed submission; on July 30, a survivor — identified only as Jane Doe, represented pro bono by attorney Brittany Henderson — moved to intervene for the limited purpose of preserving victim redactions, asking to submit her identity ex parte and under seal. That motion is the quiet heart of the case: the only survivor-protective intervention in the only courtroom fight over the files. It has never been ruled on. As of this writing it has been pending eighty-two days, and the public docket shows no disposition anywhere in its fifty-nine entries — an absence this document flags rather than explains [S-tier: docket, verified].

**August 5-13.** Phang moved to compel the overdue answer (Blanche had missed the June 26 deadline; the Department's stay motion followed minutes after); the court set a status conference for August 13, where — over roughly thirty minutes, in a transcript later placed on the public docket — Sullivan delivered the summer's defining passages. To the government's lawyer, who had little to say about redaction justifications: "The public has a right to know what the hell is going on in this case. The victims have a right to know." On the Stevens history: "I don't want to do it, but I will do it to ensure justice is fairly administered. That's not a threat. It's a promise. No one's in trouble. I'm just bringing it to your attention." And when the lawyer suggested the court could simply issue a clear order: "I've done that. Do you want me to issue another order and say, 'I really mean it this time'?" The government invoked "the presumption of regularity"; the court answered that the presumption was the Department's to forfeit. Counsel for Phang, Emily Gilman: "They just don't want to produce those documents" [S-tier: transcript #34; Courthouse News].

**August 24 - September 21.** The Department filed a protective notice of appeal — calling its own appealability "inconclusive" — to the D.C. Circuit (No. 26-5299, from the injunction). On September 16, in two opinions, the court granted the motion to compel the long-overdue answer (Blanche having "failed to establish excusable neglect," provided "no information" supporting oversight, and never addressed two of four factors; his jurisdictional challenges "without merit," having been rejected in June), denied the stay, ordered the four sets of handwritten notes — redacted and unredacted — to his chambers by September 24 at 11:00 AM, directed the Department to document its support for three victim-name redactions, ordered the foreign-language review begun, and denied three random intervention motions for Local Rule failures. The foreign-language passage bears quoting: "[Blanche] cites no authority for the proposition that administrative inconvenience excuses him from meeting his statutory obligations. Nor does he cite any authority for his apparent contention that Congressional silence in response to the January 30, 2026 letter functions as an implied amendment to the [law]." On the notes: "The underlying handwritten notes are not duplicates of the FD-302 interview reports" — Phang's side having identified nearly a hundred other released handwritten-note sets, and the Department having conceded it had, at that point, produced not a single foreign-language document. Civil contempt was "premature at this juncture," the court warned — warning being the operative word. The next day the Department appealed again (No. 26-5334). Phang's combined circuit filing asked the court to dismiss the second appeal and revealed her endgame: compelled production of "all materials that have not been produced but should be," declaratory relief, and a special master. It also quoted the Department's own FOIA boilerplate against it — letters asserting the Act "directed a much broader and less redacted release of the files than would have been made under the FOIA," and the Attorney General's concession that Phang "cannot get the documents she seeks" under FOIA. The panel — Chief Judge Srinivasan and Judges Pillard and Pan — consolidated the appeals; the Department's Friday-evening stay demand, filed at 8:45 PM with a Monday deadline over Yom Kippur and without conferring as local rules require, was denied September 21, the court self-staying only the foreign-language-review provision so the government could seek circuit relief [S-tier: Docs 42-49; Public Integrity Project filing].

**September 24 - October 16.** Blanche's answer arrived September 24 with five exhibits — the January 4 review protocol, the December 19 letter, the January 30 letter, the February 14 §3 report letter, and the August 27 Federal Register publication — and it "categorically" denied unlawful noncompliance while admitting, in its enumeration, that no files had been withheld on national-defense or foreign-policy grounds and that documents had been removed and reposted when unredacted material was discovered. The certified administrative record list came October 1; the record itself was produced to Phang by October 15. On October 1, the Department moved for "miscellaneous relief" — in substance, judgment on the pleadings to end the case "without further factual development," and a postponement of the record obligations pending that motion; the reply came October 13 (Block, 11 pages). On October 9, ECF 57 reported the in-camera review: the court accepted the Department's documentation for the three victim-identity redactions and its explanation for the fourth interview report's notes, but noted the Department had supplied only unredacted versions of three note-sets when both versions had been directed — and ordered the redacted versions to his chambers by noon on October 16. On October 16 at 3:20 PM, the notice of compliance was filed. Those notes — the handwritten originals beneath the Jane Doe 4 302s — now sit in a judge's chambers. Nothing has been ordered public [S-tier: ECF 51, 54, 55, 57, 58, 59].

## Part VI — The Money, In Full

This Part follows every documented dollar: where the money came from, whose hands it passed through, where it went, and what was purchased with it. It is the longest Part of this document because the money is where the abstractions end — not "the system," but named banks, named executives, named accounts, named prices, and documents for nearly all of it.

A word before the ledger opens, for the reader who does not live in bank statements. The money chapters work like one long courtroom exhibit, and they are best read the way a jury reads one: not by memorizing every figure, but by watching the pattern form. A bank writes a warning in its own files. Nothing happens. It writes another, sharper. Nothing happens. Years later — after the arrest, after the death, after there is no longer any client to protect or profit to lose — the bank files the report the law required all along, now covering three hundred times the money. When you see that shape repeat at four banks across two decades, you have seen the whole exhibit, and what it proves — negligence, incentive, complicity, or some blend of the three — is a question this document leaves where it belongs, with the reader and eventually with courts. The individual wires matter — each is named and sourced — and each reader will weigh them [T2 — framing; every underlying fact T1].

### Chapter 30: The Patterns

Step back, and the individual scandals resolve into seven documented patterns, each assembled from the chapters above.

**Pattern A — Post-mortem reporting.** Every major bank flagged the money only after arrest or death. JPMorgan's post-death filings covered nearly 300 times the value of its lifetime filings. **Pattern D — The Eastern corridor.** The recruitment map and the money map are one map: sanctioned banks, correspondent chains, scouts on $5,000-a-month retainers, "visa help" cover stories, agency cards, cash cadences from Palm Beach to Moscow, and the same payment channels running for years through the same aide.

### Chapter 44: The Duplicates

The Department's largest explanation for the withheld universe is duplication, and duplication is the one category the public can audit, because the released corpus contains the duplicates.

Two independent measurements exist. The New York Times built a duplicate-matching engine over the January 30 corpus — 2,683,926 pages indexed, similarity scored by combined text and image fingerprints — and found that true duplicates run roughly six to eight percent of pages: the flagged population was 630,316 pages, but validation showed only twenty-two and a half percent of the medium band were real duplicates, and the high band, even if pure, closes the arithmetic near eight percent [S-tier: arXiv 2609.30611, September 2026]. The engine's working numbers deserve preservation: 2,683,926 pages indexed from the January 30 release alone; the flagged population split into a high-similarity band of 82,417 pages and a medium band of 547,899; matching by a 449-dimension composite of text embedding and visual fingerprint at a similarity threshold of 0.92, where a four-hundred-pair human validation found precision of 0.28 against recall of 0.86 — a tool tuned to miss few duplicates at the cost of flagging many near-matches. More than one hundred journalists used the engine. And the near-matches were not merely noise: the corpus contains the same page released twice under different redaction masks, a passenger manifest redacted in one copy and visible in another, allowing independent analysts to partially reconstruct what was cut — the released record auditing itself, file by file. A second analysis, by a newsroom with an AI firm, found at least forty percent of the library duplicative — measured at the file level, where a handful of massively-replicated email chains dominate counts. Pages and files, again — the two measurements use the unit the other does not, and both agree the corpus is heavily duplicated [T1 for both figures; reconciliation labeled].

The asymmetry is the finding. The Department withheld on the duplicate theory while having released the duplicates it held — the corpus's own duplicate structure means independent analysts can partially reconstruct redactions by pairing near-duplicate pages, the same page released twice under different masks. One viral claim did not survive scrutiny and is logged as debunked: a brute-force count suggesting Dataset 10 held 686 files out of 896,000 misread the page-sequential numbering; the forensic recount proved the corpus one hundred percent complete within its boundaries, zero gaps [S/T1].

And the duplicate defense had its decisive test. The Department called the handwritten notes "duplicative of the typewritten reports." The notes, when they surfaced, contained what the typed summaries did not — travel, schools, witnesses. A federal judge ruled, in words that ended the argument: "The underlying handwritten notes are not duplicates of the FD-302 interview reports" [S-tier: Phang PI opinion and Sept. 16 order].

### Chapter 46: The Two Draft Indictments

Among everything withheld, one document outranks the rest, and its story is now complete enough to tell in full.

There were two draft federal indictments. The first — fifty-six pages, thirty-two counts, thirty against Epstein and two not naming him — was released on January 30, 2026: "old draft" scrawled across the top, six alleged victims from 2001 to 2005, three unnamed co-defendants, the fourteen enticement counts, the four travel counts, the ten trafficking counts, the conspiracy. It had never been public [S-tier: CBS's count analysis; AP].

The second — the sixty-count draft of May 2007, the one prosecutors were prepared to file on fifteen days' notice — has never been released. It exists in the public record only through grand-jury transcripts published December 23, 2025, which describe Count 51, the trafficking of "Jane Doe Number 3," the two aircraft companies named as corporate defendants, the three employees who would have been charged, and the Bureau's agent testifying before the grand jury in May 2007. Data Set 12 shipped with 152 manifested documents; sixteen were absent, and twenty more arrived outside the manifest in the March 5 backfill — the unmanifested-file problem, documentable at the data-set level [T1]. The lead prosecutor's authorship of the sixty-count draft is confirmed at the highest filing tier — her own Supreme Court petition describes "a draft 60-count indictment outlining charges against Epstein" and a lengthy memorandum [S-tier: No. 24-1073]. She learned of the work-release arrangement days after sentencing and strenuously objected; the arrangement went forward anyway [A/T1]. The original memo, the Eleventh Circuit fixed, ran eighty-two pages against the fifty-three-page indictment [S-tier]. At the negotiation table, the office identified forty victims for restitution purposes; the term of incarceration fell, in steps no one could later explain, from twenty-four months to twenty, to eighteen. Its supporting memorandum is two hundred five pages, sent to the U.S. Attorney with a request to present a sealed indictment on May 15 and a warning of "extremely high flight risk"; a four-version trail of that memo shows the case standing down across the year — the corporate defendants removed in September, travel counts cut from thirty-four to four, victims removed in February 2008 for strategic reasons, *including the original complainant whose parents had first gone to police* — and ending with the non-prosecution agreement on June 30, 2008 [S/T1: EFTA IDs across the arc]. The memo's own pages preserve what the negotiation destroyed: nineteen identified victims aged fourteen to seventeen; two hundred twenty-five enumerated overt acts; forfeiture of the Palm Beach mansion, the Boeing 727, and the Gulfstream, all three named with their holding companies; an early-1990s New Mexico ranch incident in which Epstein and Maxwell, per the memorandum, "fondled the girl," after which a family member was threatened and Maxwell told one of them she "could just disappear"; a Vanity Fair reporter "approached by someone from Epstein's camp" who became so fearful for her own safety and her young child's that the piece was rewritten to remove all references to sexual conduct, and who refused to speak to the FBI; the assistant who, after federal agents attempted to interview her, went upstairs "claiming she needed to change her baby" but instead called Epstein, who rerouted his flight to evade agents preparing to serve target letters — after which, in the memo's words, Epstein's team "began negotiating in earnest"; and an immunity demand from a cooperating witness whose attorney sought full protection, prompting the prosecutor's observation that approximately ninety percent of the identified girls had brought other girls, so all of them would feel at risk, and the note that Epstein had "shipped off" another potential cooperator to the Middle East. The memo records, too, that Epstein offered victims David Copperfield tickets, that one victim spoke with Copperfield by telephone, and that a message-pad call from Copperfield sat in the files [S/T1: EFTA02857524].

The Eleventh Circuit fixed the original pagination forever: by May 2007 the office had completed an eighty-two-page prosecution memo and a fifty-three-page draft indictment [S-tier: 19-13843, April 14, 2020]. And the Office of Professional Responsibility's full record, captured in its own words, supplies the deal's anatomy: the supervisor's instruction that the indictment "needs to be ultra lean with only clean victims" — he explained to investigators that clean meant victims without impeachment problems; the lead prosecutor's suppressed request to make a presentation that "will convince you all of the strength of the case," answered by a rebuke that her tone was "highly unacceptable"; the OPR's own heading for what no one could explain — the evidence does not clearly show why the term of incarceration was reduced from twenty-four months to twenty, to eighteen; her guidelines calculation of 188 to 235 months, presented at the table; the concession that the unsearched 2005 computers might have held "very powerful evidence" that "possibly could have changed our advice... or his decision making"; the victim letters sent months after the federal case was resolved, telling victims the case "is currently under investigation," which the OPR found "risked misleading"; and the two protective terms the defense stripped — the deal limited to already-identified victims, and a one-month acceptance window [S-tier: OPR full text].

The agreement itself, captured verbatim from its 2024 unsealing, carries the mechanism future readers will quote: the victim representative selected with the defense's good-faith approval and paid by Epstein; the gain-time clause; and paragraph thirteen, the secrecy clause — "The parties anticipate that this agreement will not be made part of any public record. If the United States receives a Freedom of Information Act request... it will provide notice to Epstein before making that disclosure" [S-tier].

And the post-agreement record deepens it: an August 2008 email chain among the U.S. Attorney and his office discussing "a possible future agreement with Epstein that might supersede the 2007 NPA" — eleven months after signing, while he was incarcerated and challenging the deal; a June 2008 memo, nine months post-agreement, still memorializing the case against Epstein and five assistants; and a September email from the lead prosecutor proposing to meet defense counsel "off campus" with all decision-makers "on call," five days before the signing [S/T1 as tagged]. At the September meeting that settled the framework, Ken Starr addressed himself directly to the U.S. Attorney and argued federalism, adding — in the OPR's record — that he and Acosta were "the only people in this room who have run the gantlet of confirmation by the Senate." The defense appealed the terms to the Deputy Attorney General's office, which declined to intervene on June 23, 2008; only then did Epstein accept the deal's obligations. And eleven months after the signing, with Epstein incarcerated and challenging the agreement, an August 2008 email chain among Acosta and his own office discussed "a possible future agreement with Epstein that might supersede the 2007 NPA" — the parties still negotiating at the very top of the Department long after the case was supposed to be closed. ### Chapter 47: The Leak

The thirty-seven pages reached the public by a documented chain, and the chain says as much about the system as the pages say about the case.

Twenty-two of the thirty-seven were uploaded to a Scribd account traced to Washington Examiner justice reporter Kaelan Deese on March 6, 2026 — one day before his article on what was still missing from the files, without any mention of what he held. For five months, anyone on the internet could read the accuser's real name, her biographical details, and the names of her childhood friends and family — everything she had given the FBI in confidence, everything the Department had redacted — until the forensic researcher who traced the account contacted his editor, and the files came down. Deese had also transmitted additional notes to "one random person" over Signal; the researcher's team found that person, who produced the exchanges. The attribution evidence is documented and reviewed; the researcher describes it as significant though not government-authenticated [S/T1: Document Zero reporting; the Beltway account].

The researcher who verified the documents states their provenance plainly: the reporter "was part of a small, select group of people that DOJ brass secretly decided were — unlike the public and members of Congress — able to be trusted with these allegedly 'duplicative,' ultra-sensitive files. Entirely unredacted." A trusted-outsider distribution of unredacted files ran parallel to both the redacted public release and the seventy-to-eighty-percent-redacted congressional reading room. Who else held that trust remains open; what is documented is the system's existence.

The two members who walked that reading room had asked to see three specific categories, in a January 30 letter to the Deputy Attorney General: the hundreds of thousands of pages of emails recovered from Epstein's accounts during the 2018 and 2019 investigations; the victim-interview statements from the Florida and New York investigations, which they observed consisted "largely of pages that are fully redacted"; and the fifty-three-page draft indictment with the eighty-two-page prosecution memorandum [T1: the Khanna-Massie letter]. And the Department's response to the leak of its own supposedly too-sensitive-to-release material is a documented negative: two targeted searches found no statement, referral, or investigation [T1].

The same release waves carried a documented fabrication, which the Department itself authenticated as fake: a handwritten letter appearing to be from Epstein to Larry Nassar, postmarked in Virginia three days after Epstein died, in a hand that did not match — proof, the Department's own account noted, that release does not make a document's contents factual. Alongside it, an Epstein-authored email defaming Bill Gates, which a spokesman called "absolutely absurd and completely false." The corpus contains forgeries and fabrications in both directions; both are logged [T1].

### Chapter 48: The Foreign-Language Void

One withheld category has never been quantified by anyone, and its absence of quantification is itself the finding.

The January 30 letter concedes it in a sentence: the collection "included pages in various foreign languages," and "because it was not practicable for a first-level reviewer to determine the responsiveness of a foreign-language document, those pages have not been produced" — alongside "materials that could not be uploaded for review... because of technical issues with the files." No count. No languages. No custodians. No plan [S-tier].

The court treated it as what it was: the only category where the plaintiff took full mandatory relief, an order to initiate review and production. The Department defied the order for nearly three months without seeking a stay, relitigating it at a hearing where the judge asked whether he should issue another order and say, "I really mean it this time." On appeal, the Department's brief argued the stay was needed because "disclosed information cannot later be made undisclosed." The D.C. Circuit has the question under a narrow administrative freeze — the stay covers only this slice, with a joint notice due three days after it rules. As of the last verified date, not a single foreign-language document has been publicly produced. The appellate filing notes the pending follow-on statute "confirms that he was required to publish a full redaction log, not just a summary" [S/T1: Phang docket; Public Integrity Project filing].

### Chapter 49: The Taxonomy of Withholding

Why haven't the remaining pages been released? The Department has given four different official lists, and none of them matches the statute's five permitted grounds.

The press release named four: duplicates between the two U.S. Attorney's offices; privilege; the depictions-of-violence exception; and items "completely unrelated" to the cases. The January 30 letter named six, adding the foreign-language and technically-incompatible materials, and a sixth — materials a law firm produced under grand-july subpoena in the Maxwell investigation, encumbered by a civil protective order; the letter said the Department "would be prudent to seek an order from the court" before releasing them. The order had already been obtained four months earlier, on December 9, 2025, when a federal judge modified the protective order to let the Act override it, subject to victim-information redactions and a personal certification by the U.S. Attorney. Whether those materials were ever released is unverified — the category outlived its own resolution. The same December 9 order supplies the most complete official inventory of what that encumbered discovery contains, twenty-six categories recited from the bench: travel records (commercial airlines, private passenger logs, manifests); grand-jury subpoena returns from the internet providers (Instagram, Microsoft, Google); agency records from a dozen bodies; school records; search-warrant returns including computer and phone extractions; materials produced by law firms, including firms representing victims; materials from related civil litigations including deposition recordings; immigration records; online-purchase records; Palm Beach police and prosecutor reports, photographs, and video; forensic examination reports; postal and courier records; estate materials; driver's-license records; and the interview reports and notes of third parties and victims. It is the composition map of a major slice of the privilege-withheld universe, read aloud in a ruling [S-tier: Engelmayer Dec. 9 order]. A social-media post named three. The statute names five, and "technical issues" has no statutory home in any of them [T1 — internal inconsistency].

The why beneath the whys remains, as this document has insisted from its first Part, a question the record poses but does not answer. A forensic researcher added a coda to the "technical issues" category: the unproduced files, he found, were often not corrupted documents at all but raw disk-imaging artifacts packaged as PDFs — and inside five of them, byte-level analysis recovered eight contact records; an iPhone 5s photograph of the island temple, its embedded timestamp reading August 3, 2014; a memo from W. Chester Brewer Jr. confirming the Epstein-v.-Rothstein litigation's paper trail; and a San Mateo probate order for the Elisa Zaffaroni trust. The pipeline's loss was partial; what else sits in the unopened sectors, no one has counted [A/T1: Howard-Stone forensic analysis].

And the states weighed in: seventeen attorneys general, in an October 15, 2026 letter urging the follow-on statute's passage, put the official characterization in writing — "millions of files remain withheld, often due to claims of legal privilege. Yet, the personally identifying information of dozens of victims has been disclosed despite the attempted safeguards." The report letter itself, the six-page list of officials and politically exposed persons, was signed by both the Attorney General and the Deputy Attorney General, and carried the Department's own caveats — the term "politically exposed person" appears nowhere in the law, the contexts ranged from extensive email correspondence to passing mention in press clippings, and the list reached for the dead (Elvis Presley, Princess Diana, Marilyn Monroe, Janis Joplin) alongside the living [S/T1]. The estate instrument itself had been updated and amended more than two dozen times across the final years — a document in constant motion [T1].

The Department says volume, duplicates, victim protection, and privilege. A federal judge found the Attorney General in violation of the Act. The evidence of suppression is documented — altered documents, the surveillance of the overseers, the real-time unredactions, the trusted-outsider pool, the unreconciled error figures a hundred times apart. The competing account — error, haste, an understaffed review drowning in six million pages — is also documented: the mid-stream guidance changes, the volume metrics, the coding error no one has claimed. What the withheld two and a half million pages contain, divided by category, the Department has never itemized. That itemization is the single accounting this Part cannot supply, because no one outside the building has the ledger — and the ledger documents now named in the record ("Epstein Redactions Final Page Count"; "List of Absent Items") have not yet surfaced from the rolling production where they sit [T1/T2 as labeled throughout].
